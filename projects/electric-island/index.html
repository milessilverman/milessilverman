02 / The project

An idea with a purpose.

A home battery can help one household during a blackout. Electric Island asks what happens when those resources can support an entire block. Switches at homes, transformers, and utility poles let the grid split into islands of any size: one house, fifteen, or three hundred. Each island runs on home batteries, EVs, and solar, switches automatically, and keeps a simple manual override.

Resilience at the scale of a block.

Our team developed the concept in 48 hours and pitched it at the Dual-Use Hackathon, taking first place among 60 teams. Afterward, I built and deployed an interactive grid simulator to explore the idea further and reviewed prior-art patents to help define its novelty.

A reason for every home to opt in.

Each island’s main battery keeps a bottom reserve for emergencies, about 10 kWh, or more where a site wants it, for example 50 kWh. The rest charges on cheap or excess power and feeds the grid when electricity is expensive, so owners earn the most by giving power back when it is scarcest. EV battery capacity is saved for emergencies.

Where it applies.

Only new homes get nodes, so the system grows with new construction instead of retrofits. Military and research bases sit at the end of the grid and are supported by islands upstream and downstream of them.

What is new in it.

The closest prior art is the University of Tennessee’s dynamic-boundary microgrids, GridOn’s hierarchical sub-microgrids, and S&C’s transformer islanding. The defensible idea is the combination: at every level, opening never needs permission and closing passes every check, plus load caps tied to battery frequency so storage is never drained past its reserve.
